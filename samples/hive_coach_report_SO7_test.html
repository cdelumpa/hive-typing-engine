1A · Counter-Type Considerations
Why This Matters
  • SO 7 (Sacrifice) is the most Type 2-resembling counter-type in the system — if you open with 'Seven' without framing, the client's first response is almost certainly going to be 'that doesn't sound like me at all.' The counter-type frame has to precede the type name.
  • The client's Stage 0 language ('selfless,' 'devotion,' 'burnout') reinforces this: they have built a strong self-concept around giving and sacrifice. Presenting a type whose standard description emphasizes personal pleasure-seeking can feel not just wrong but insulting if not carefully framed.
  • The clinical value of naming SO 7 correctly is high: it surfaces the fear of limitation and deprivation that is driving the tireless giving, which opens the conversation about what the client is actually running toward — and what they're not letting themselves stop for.
  • If the client resonates with Seven once the counter-type frame is in place, the growth edge (moving toward Five's depth and stillness in security) is likely to feel like relief rather than loss — which is a powerful place to work from.
Standard vs. Counter-Type Presentation
  • Standard Seven: pleasure-seeking, variety-oriented, keeps options open, reframes pain into future possibility, explicitly self-referential about wanting to feel good and free. The client will not see themselves here.
  • SO 7 (Sacrifice): has redirected Seven's pleasure-seeking energy entirely into group service, causes, and collective vision. The self feels most alive in giving, in inspiring, in being tireless for something larger. Personal pleasure feels almost suspect — the counter-type move is against the standard Seven pattern.
  • What they share: the Frustration triad's chronic reaching (there's always something more, something bigger), the Head center's forward-facing orientation, Positive Outlook's instinct to reframe difficulty, and the stress-to-1 / security-to-5 architecture. These are the structural anchors that confirm Seven even when the surface looks like Two.
  • Key motivational distinction: the SO 7 gives in service of a vision and the aliveness that vision generates; the Two gives in service of love and the belonging that giving secures. Both can produce identical behavior; the inner organizer is different.
Coaching Notes
  • Open with the phenomenon before the label: 'There's a version of Seven that has completely redirected its energy into causes and community — where the vision and the giving feel like the most alive thing. Does any of that resonate before we go further?'
  • If the client is skeptical, anchor in the structural evidence they can verify: 'When you're under stress, do you notice yourself becoming more critical and perfectionistic — holding yourself and others to a higher standard than you normally would?' The stress-to-1 confirmation is your most concrete foothold.
  • Give the client full permission to hold both Seven and Two as live possibilities. The counter-type framing is an invitation to look underneath the surface presentation, not an insistence on a label. The question 'what is the giving in service of?' is more generative than 'which type are you.'
2 · Debriefing Core Motivation and Worldview

How to present the heart of the type and connect it to their own words.

The Core Pattern
  • Type 7's core worldview is organized around deprivation: the world is a place where limitation, pain, and loss are real threats, and the fundamental strategy is to stay ahead of them by keeping possibility open, moving toward stimulating and meaningful experience, and reframing difficulty before it can fully land.
  • In SO 7 this forward-reaching drive is fully socialized: the client doesn't seek personal pleasure — they seek the aliveness of vision, cause, and collective uplift. The giving is real; it is also doing the work of keeping limitation at bay by always having something vital to move toward.
  • The Frustration triad signal is important here: Sevens (along with Ones and Fours) experience a chronic dissatisfaction with what is actually available, a sense that what they need is always just over the horizon. For SO 7, the cause is the horizon — and 'burnout' is what happens when the body finally encounters the limitation the mind has been outrunning.
  • The growth question for Seven is learning to stay with limitation, loss, and depth without escaping into the next thing — which, in the SO 7 expression, means learning that the cause can wait, that stillness is not deprivation, and that going inward can be as alive as going forward.
What Their Responses Showed
  • The phrase 'inspire people toward something bigger than themselves' is the clearest Seven signal in the dataset: it is forward-facing, expansive, and vision-organized rather than relationally intimate. This is not how a Two typically frames their greatest strength.
  • 'Tireless' is consistent with Seven's characteristic high energy and difficulty registering limitation — the word choice implies that stopping is not in the vocabulary until 'burnout' arrives as an external wall.
  • 'Devoted to the people and causes I care about' is worth sitting with — both 'people' and 'causes' are named together. In a Two, people typically come first; the cause is secondary. Here they appear co-equal, which is consistent with SO 7's group-and-vision orientation.
  • 'Always there — sometimes to a fault' from others is an SO instinct signal: attunement to group need is so strong it overrides the client's own self-referential awareness — which is both the Seven counter-type's gift and the place where the fear of limitation is least visible.
Coaching Notes
  • When presenting the worldview, start with the experiential texture — 'What does it feel like when you imagine slowing down, or when a cause you've been pouring yourself into suddenly isn't there?' — before naming the structural fear of deprivation. Let them locate it in their body first.
  • Watch for explanation when you name the fear dimension: SO 7 clients often move quickly into justifying why their giving is genuinely meaningful (which it is) when what's being invited is a look at what's underneath the drive. When explanation shows up, consider gently naming it: 'I notice we moved into the reasons this matters — what's happening inside when I point toward the part that's running from something?'
  • The burnout detail is a gift — it is the place where the worldview has already failed, which means the client has direct experiential access to what the structure costs them. Start there.
Try asking: 'When you imagine a season with no big cause to throw yourself into — nothing pressing, nowhere to be tireless — what comes up for you?'
