1A · Counter-Type Considerations
Why This Matters
  • The SO 7 counter-type is one of the most commonly mistyped patterns in the system — it looks so much like Type 2 that even experienced practitioners can miss it. The behavioral presentation (selfless, devoted, always there, service-oriented) is almost identical on the surface. Naming the counter-type explicitly — and explaining why — will help this client engage with their type rather than feel misfiled.
  • If you lead with the standard Seven description (adventure, pleasure, variety, reframing pain), this client will likely push back or disengage — not because they're not a Seven, but because the counter-type suppresses exactly those qualities. The counter-type is not a variation on Seven; it is a specific expression of Seven's core motivation through an entirely different behavioral channel.
  • The Sacrifice name is itself a diagnostic: the SO 7 sacrifices their own pleasure and comfort in service of a shared vision or cause. The devotion is real — and it is also the Seven's way of keeping life full of meaning and staying ahead of the emptiness they fear. That layered read is what makes this type so rich to work with.
  • Stress and security architecture confirmed cleanly for Seven — that is your strongest evidence base and the most useful place to anchor recognition when you need to.
Standard vs. Counter-Type Presentation
  • Standard Seven: pleasure-seeking, future-oriented, keeps options open, reframes pain into possibility, energized by variety and new experiences. The fear of limitation and emptiness is met by filling life with exciting prospects.
  • SO 7 Sacrifice: the same fear of limitation and emptiness is met by pouring oneself into a cause or community larger than oneself. The devotion, the tirelessness, the vision for what is possible — all of these are the Seven's core drive expressed through complete identification with a shared purpose.
  • What they share: the Frustration Object Relations stance (the gap between what is and what could be that drives idealism and forward motion), the Positive Outlook harmony style (approaching difficulty by orienting toward possibility and inspiring others), and the underlying dread of a life that feels small, empty, or meaningless.
  • What distinguishes them motivationally: ask whether the driving force underneath the giving is about keeping life rich with meaning and possibility (Seven) or about securing love, connection, and indispensability through giving (Two). The SO 7 will often have a harder time answering this than a standard Two — both feel true — which is exactly why the structural data matters here.
Coaching Notes
  • Introduce the counter-type framing directly but curiously: 'I want to offer something that might initially feel surprising — the pattern that showed up most strongly actually belongs to a type that doesn't usually look like this.' Give them permission to be surprised.
  • Use the client's own language to bridge from counter-type presentation to the underlying Seven motivation: 'You described yourself as tireless and devoted to inspiring people toward something bigger. I want to ask you: what does it feel like when you can't do that? When the cause stalls, or the people you're serving don't need you anymore — what happens in there?'
  • If they resist the Seven frame, do not defend it — invite them into the structural data (stress toward One, security toward Five) and let the architecture speak. Most clients find stress/security movements more accessible than idealized type descriptions.
2 · Debriefing Core Motivation and Worldview

How to present the heart of the type and connect it to their own words.

The Core Pattern
  • The Seven's core motivation is organized around a fear of deprivation, limitation, and inner emptiness — and a corresponding drive to keep life full of meaning, possibility, and forward motion. This is not primarily about pleasure; it is about the terror of a life that feels small or foreclosed.
  • In the SO 7 counter-type, this drive is channeled through the social instinct: the way you keep life feeling full and meaningful is by dedicating yourself to something larger — a vision, a cause, a community. The fullness comes through contribution and shared purpose rather than personal pleasure.
  • The idealization pattern is 'I am someone who gives myself fully to what matters' — the Seven who has transformed their hunger for aliveness into a capacity for devotion. This is genuinely beautiful, and it is also a coping structure: the devotion keeps the Seven oriented toward what is possible rather than toward what is missing or painful.
  • Burnout is the Seven's limitation arriving through the back door. The direct experience of emptiness or deprivation is avoided — but the physical and emotional cost of running without stopping becomes the limitation that can't be ignored.
What Their Responses Showed
  • 'Devoted to the people and causes I care about, tireless' — this is the SO 7 idealization in precise language: the self who keeps going, who gives fully, who has oriented everything outward toward something meaningful.
  • 'Inspire people toward something bigger than themselves' named as greatest strength — this is the Frustration Object Relations stance in action: the gap between what is and what could be, channeled into vision and mobilization of others. This language would be unusual for a Type 2, whose strength language more often centers on care, attunement, and making individuals feel seen.
  • 'Sometimes to a fault' in others' description, followed by 'burnout' as the problematic quality — both point to the same underlying pattern: the system does not have a natural off switch, and limitation arrives through collapse rather than through conscious choice.
  • 'Selfless, visionary, always there' from others — the 'visionary' quality in particular is distinctive and worth holding: it suggests a forward-facing, possibility-oriented cast of mind that is more consistent with Seven than with Two.
Coaching Notes
  • This client is likely comfortable talking about their devotion and vision — that is the idealized self-image speaking. The more interesting coaching territory is underneath that: what does the inner world look like when the vision isn't enough, when the cause disappoints, when the people they're serving can't receive what they're offering?
  • When you introduce the core motivation, watch whether the client can locate the fear of emptiness or limitation in their own experience. The SO 7 often experiences this as 'there's no time to stop' or 'I can't afford to slow down' rather than as fear per se — so soften the language accordingly.
  • The burnout thread is the most direct entry point into the core motivation conversation — it is the place where the Seven's avoidance pattern becomes visible without requiring the client to name fear directly.
Try asking: 'When you imagine a season where there is no cause to give yourself to, no one particularly needing what you offer — what comes up? What does that feel like in your body?'

The 'tireless' self-image is replaced by a harsh internal judge that catalogs everything that isn't good enough.

Security Movement — Toward Type 5
  • In security, Sevens access Type Five's positive qualities — the capacity to slow down, go deep, become focused and absorbed in one thing without needing to immediately move on. For a client who describes themselves as 'tireless,' this is a profound resource: the Seven at rest discovers that stillness is not emptiness but depth.
  • This client confirmed the Five security movement strongly (BOTH_MORE_A), which is significant. Ask them about moments when they have genuinely slowed down and gone deep — what made that possible, what it felt like, and what got in the way of sustaining it.
  • The coaching angle: the path through burnout for this client likely runs through the Five security point — through learning to trust stillness, to let life be less full, to discover that depth is a form of aliveness that doesn't require constant motion.
Try asking: 'Think of a time when you were able to really slow down and go deep into something — fully present, not needing to move on. What was happening? What made that possible?'
Wings — Type 6 and Type 8
  • Type 7 wings are Type 6 (the Loyalist) and Type 8 (the Challenger). Both are worth exploring with this client, and the wings conversation often opens naturally once the type is established.
  • The 6 wing brings increased loyalty, anxiety about whether the cause will hold, care for the group's security and stability, and a capacity for greater commitment and follow-through. For an SO 7, a 6 wing can amplify the devotional quality — but also the anxiety about letting people down.
  • The 8 wing brings directness, energy, a willingness to confront, and a stronger sense of personal power. For an SO 7 with an 8 wing, the devotion has more edge — they will fight for what they believe in and can become genuinely formidable when the cause is threatened.
  • Watch which wing language lands more readily — and listen for whether the client's stress description (becoming critical and perfectionistic toward One) shows up with Eight-wing anger underneath it, or with Six-wing anxiety underneath it.
Try asking: 'When you're advocating hard for something you believe in — do you find yourself more in a fighting stance, willing to confront, or more in a worrying stance, checking whether everyone is still on board? Which feels more like you?'
6 · If the Conversation Goes Sideways

What to do depending on how they receive the hypothesis.

If They Resonate Strongly
  • If this client strongly recognizes the pattern — especially the tirelessness, the devotion, the burnout, and the inner critic under stress — the most useful move is to slow down the recognition and turn it toward commitment. Recognition without commitment is just interesting information.
  • Use the recognition as a launching point for the what's-underneath conversation: 'You said that lands. So I want to ask you — what do you notice when you sit with this? Not the pattern itself, but what it's protecting. What would it feel like to stop?'
Try asking: 'Of everything we've talked about, what is the one thing you most want to understand better — and what would it mean for your life if you could shift it?'
If They Push Back or Disagree
  • Do not defend the hypothesis. If the client pushes back, the most likely reason is either (a) they don't recognize themselves in Seven at all because the counter-type description wasn't clear enough, or (b) they feel more resonance with Type 2 and want to explore that.
  • The most useful response to pushback is curiosity: 'That's really useful — tell me what doesn't fit.' Listen carefully for whether they're describing a motivation (I give because I need to feel loved and needed) or a drive (I give because life feels meaningful when I'm contributing to something larger). That distinction is the key.
  • If they lean toward Type 2: honor it fully, name the very real overlap, and then offer the structural data gently — 'The thing I noticed is how you described moving under stress — toward self-criticism and perfectionism rather than toward anger and control. That's a Type 7 movement. I'd like to offer that and see what you think.' Let them decide.

Most likely alternate type: Type 2 — The Helper

Key distinguishing question: The key distinguishing question is: does the devotion and giving arise primarily from a drive to keep life full of meaning and possibility (Seven), or from a need to be needed, loved, and seen as indispensable by the people you serve (Two)?

If They're Confused or Need More Clarity
  • If the client is confused — genuinely uncertain whether Seven or Two fits — treat the confusion as the most honest and useful place to work from. Name both patterns plainly, describe what is genuinely shared, and describe the one place they diverge at the motivational root.
  • Find the foothold: use the stress and security movements as the most concrete and accessible evidence. 'Let me ask you to try something: think about a recent period when you were most stressed. When you got there, did you get angrier and more controlling — or more self-critical and perfectionistic?' The answer to that question is often the clearest data available.
  • Treat what doesn't fit as equally useful as what does: 'The fact that the helper description feels so true is important — tell me about that. And the fact that something about it also doesn't quite fit — tell me about that too.'
Try asking: 'When you imagine no longer being needed by the causes and people you serve — what is the first thing you notice? Is it closer to relief, grief, loss of love, or loss of meaning?'
6A · Type Confusion Observation Block

Types in question: Type 7 and Type 2. Use only if the client brought in their type confusion observation during the session.

What to Do With What They Bring
  • Bring the Habit of Mind data into the conversation directly but lightly: 'One thing I noticed is that when we asked about where your attention naturally goes, your answers were more about tracking others' needs and seeking connection and approval — which is actually more of a Type 2 attention pattern. For the Two, attending to others is the emotional center of gravity — it is where they feel most alive and most secure. The language around 'why' they track others will carry the diagnostic signal.
  • Hold both types with genuine respect and let the client lead: 'Both of these patterns are present in your responses in different ways. I want to offer them both and see which feels closer to the bone — not the behavior, but the thing underneath the behavior that makes it necessary.'
If the Observation Didn't Yield Clear Data
  • The SO 7 counter-type is notoriously difficult to confirm through self-report alone because the counter-type behavior suppresses the most recognizable Seven signals. If the conversation doesn't yield clarity, it is not a failure of the client's self-awareness — it is a known limitation of the assessment format for this particular type.
  • Type 2 is similarly difficult to confirm through self-report because the Two's core need (to be loved and needed) is often experienced as simply 'being who I am' — the need is so naturalized that it doesn't register as a need. A client who is a Two may experience their giving as pure devotion, which makes it nearly indistinguishable from the SO 7's account.
  • If no data resolves the question, consider ending the session with a between-session observation rather than a conclusion: 'I'd invite you to notice this week — in the moments when you're most giving, most devoted, most in it — what is the quality of that experience? Is there a sense of freedom and aliveness in it, or is there a quiet vigilance underneath it, checking whether you are being received and valued?'
Try asking: 'When you do something generous for someone you care about and they don't notice, or don't respond the way you hoped — what happens inside? Is it closer to disappointment, or something more like being cut loose from something you needed?'
