  • When the client describes a moment of unusual clarity or directness, consider asking what that energy felt like and whether they recognize it as something they have access to.
The Lines
Stress Line: Type 1 — The Perfectionist

Under stress, Sevens can move toward Type 1 territory — becoming more critical, perfectionistic, or rigidly focused on getting things right. The energy is sharper and more constrained, often accompanied by frustration that things aren't going as planned. When this shows up, it's usually a sign that the Seven's expansive options have collapsed and they're trying to control through standards instead of avoid through movement.

Security Line: Type 5 — The Observer

In moments of ease, Sevens can access Type 5 territory — becoming more focused, more willing to go deep into one thing rather than skimming many, and more able to tolerate stillness. The energy is quieter, more contained, more present. This isn't a departure from being a Seven — it's the Seven discovering that depth can be its own form of abundance.

Coaching Notes — Lines
  • The line conversation is most useful when grounded in real moments from the client's life — recall a time when you felt unusually critical or unusually focused; what was happening?
  • For this client specifically, the Type 5 security line is genuinely good news — it means depth and stillness are accessible, not foreign. The burnout pattern depletes the Seven's normal scattered-aliveness, but stillness in service of one focused thing can refill the well in a way more activity cannot.
  • The Type 1 stress line is where the Seven's reframing breaks down. When the client describes themselves as "running on fumes and getting snippy with everyone," that's the Type 1 territory showing up.
Try asking: "Can you recall a recent moment when you felt unusually focused — like everything else fell away and you were just present with one thing? What was the energy of that moment, and what did you do with it?"

6A · Type Confusion Observation Block
The Client Has Been Given an Observation Exercise

Because the data showed real ambiguity between Type 7 and Type 2, the client report includes an in-life observation exercise. The client has been invited to notice, between now and the session, what's actually driving them in challenging moments — and to compare it against the core motivations of both types.

Type 7's core motivation: to be free and full of possibility by keeping options open. Type 2's core motivation: to be loved and needed by attuning to others.

What to Do With What They Bring
  • Open the session by asking what the client observed. Treat the answer as data, not as a verdict.
  • Listen for the register of motivation in the examples they share. Was the underlying drive about not missing out / staying open to what could be (Seven) or about being needed by specific people / securing connection (Two)?
  • The distinguishing question is whether the client experiences themselves as belonging to a vision or idea (Social Seven) or as indispensable to specific people (Two).
  • Watch for examples where the client says they were helping someone — and ask what they imagined would happen if that person didn't need them. The Two will name something about disconnection or being unloved; the Social Seven is more likely to name something about not having a role in the larger picture.
If the Observation Didn't Yield Clear Data
  • This is itself diagnostic. Sevens — including Social Sevens — often have diffuse access to their own motivations because the head-center planning and reframing keeps them several steps removed from the felt sense of why they're doing what they're doing.
  • If the client struggles to remember a challenging moment, or can only describe what happened externally rather than what was driving them internally, that's information about the Seven's distance from inner experience — not a failure of the exercise.
  • Don't push for a definitive answer. Hold both possibilities and let the conversation develop. The clarification often happens over weeks, not in a single debrief.
Probe for session: "What did you notice about what was driving you when things got hard? What surprised you?"
